Flipping Pancakes

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: a messy stack of pancakes, all different sizes. Tidy it up so the biggest is at the bottom and they get smaller all the way to the top.
How to play: click a pancake to slide a flipper underneath it; everything from there upwards turns over in one go. Do it in as few flips as you can.
Look for the math

This is a real research problem in disguise. It reached the American Mathematical Monthly in 1975 from someone signing himself "Harry Dweighter" — say it out loud — and mathematicians have been chipping away at it ever since. It is known as pancake sorting.

Every mess can be tidied

Call the flip that starts under pancake i the move Mi. A stack is just a permutation of the pancakes, and the question is whether the moves can reach every one of them. They can: flipping the same spot twice undoes it, and the page shows how to build a plain swap of two neighbours out of four flips,

(i i+1) = Mi+1 Mi Mi+1 Mi+2

and neighbour swaps alone can build any rearrangement at all. That also tells you why the stack you are given is always solvable: it was made by flipping a tidy stack at random, so flipping those same places in reverse order puts it back.

A method, and how good it is

Here is a strategy that always works. Find the biggest pancake that is in the wrong place, flip it to the top, then flip it straight back down onto its proper spot. Two flips put one pancake away for good, so n pancakes take at most 2n − 3 flips. Going the other way, count the pairs of pancakes that are already neighbours in the right order; a single flip can create at most one new such pair, which forces at least n flips in the worst case once n ≥ 4. So the true worst case f(n) sits between them:

n ≤ f(n) ≤ 2n − 3

Nobody knows the answer

Checking every stack by computer gives the first few exactly:

f(2..8) = 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9

and there the trouble starts: no formula for f(n) is known. The best bounds are still estimates. In 1979 a graduate student named Bill Gates, with Christos Papadimitriou, narrowed it to 17n/16 < f(n) < 5n/3 — it remains his only published mathematics paper — and Heydari and Sudborough pushed the lower end to 15n/14 in 1997.

Try the two switches. "Flip reaches" fixes how many pancakes turn over at a time — set it to 2 and you are left with neighbour swaps, which is bubble sort. "Swap ends only" turns the flip into a plain exchange of the two ends and leaves the filling untouched; the counting changes completely.
