Dominoes on a Chessboard

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: fill the board with dominoes so that no straight line runs all the way across between them. A line nothing crosses turns red and is called a fault.
How to play: click a square, then a square next to it, to lay a domino across the two. Click a domino to take it away. Some boards cannot be done at all — the 6 by 6 is the famous one.
Look for the math

A domino covers two squares that share an edge. Covering a board with them is easy; covering it so that no grid line survives uncrossed is not. Bogomolny's page sets one exercise: prove that every covering of the 6 by 6 board leaves a fault line. The whole proof is counting.

One domino across a line is never enough

Take an interior line of the 6 by 6 and look at the board on one side of it: a 6 by t rectangle, which has an even number of squares whatever t is. Now suppose exactly one domino straddled the line. That side would then hold a whole number of dominoes plus one half — an odd number of squares — which an even rectangle cannot be. So crossings come in pairs:

every interior line needs at least 2 dominoes crossing it.

The argument works on any line that leaves an even number of squares on each side of it, which on the 6 by 6 means all ten of them.

Why the 6 by 6 cannot be done

A 6 by 6 board has 5 horizontal and 5 vertical interior lines, so 10 in all. Two dominoes each makes 20 dominoes, and every domino crosses only one line, so no domino is counted twice. Those 20 dominoes would cover 40 squares — on a board that has 36. The covering cannot exist.

The board gets very close, though. There are 6728 ways to cover a 6 by 6 with dominoes, and the best of them leaves exactly one fault line. Every one of those coverings has been checked by the test that ships with this page, and no line in any of them is crossed by exactly one domino, just as the argument above says.

Which boards do work

The page states the general rule: a fault-free covering of an N by M board exists exactly when

N·M is even, N > 4, M > 4, and the board is not the 6 by 6.

So the smallest one that works is 5 by 6. The thin boards fail for the same counting reason as the 6 by 6, only sooner, and an odd number of squares cannot be covered at all. Every board up to 7 by 8 has been searched exhaustively here, and the search agrees with the rule in every case.

Try 5 by 6 first, then the 6 by 6: get it down to a single fault line and you have matched the best covering there is.
