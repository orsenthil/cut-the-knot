        Look for the math

        Lewis Carroll, the author of Alice, was also a logician. His Game of Logic turns a syllogism, two premises and a conclusion, into counters on a board, so that the reasoning becomes a mechanical move.

        Two attributes, four boxes

        A square split into four boxes holds everything there is, sorted by two attributes: x or not x (rows), m or not m (columns). A red counter in a box means "some things are like this", a grey one "nothing is", a red counter on a line "at least one of these two". "All m are x" is two counters: red in x m and grey in not-x m.

        Three attributes, eight boxes

        The middle diagram adds the third attribute as an inner square: inside is m, outside is not m. Each premise box is split in two there. A grey box makes both halves grey; a red box becomes a red counter on the line between the halves, because we don't know which half holds the something. When a later premise greys one half, that red counter slides into the other half. This is a Venn diagram in a square.

        Eliminating the middle term

        To read off a conclusion about x and y, forget the inner square: each quadrant of the conclusion is red if either half has a red counter, and grey if both halves are grey. That is the whole method. Checking it against brute force, over all 5,041 consistent pairs of premises and every one of the 256 ways things could be, shows it is exactly right: every counter in the conclusion is forced by the premises, and everything the premises force shows up.

        "Try an example" loads Carroll's: no professors are ignorant, all ignorant people are vain. What follows about professors and vanity?
