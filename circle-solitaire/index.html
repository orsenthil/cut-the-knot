Solitaire on a Circle

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: clear the whole circle. Clicking a green counter takes it away — and turns over the counter on each side of it, so green becomes red and red becomes green.
How to play: click any green counter. Red ones can't be clicked, so if no green is left you are stuck. Watch out: some circles can't be cleared at all, however well you play.
Look for the math

This is the rare puzzle you can lose before you touch it. The counters are dealt red or green by a coin flip, and roughly half of the circles you are given cannot be cleared no matter what you do. There is a test that tells you which, and it takes one glance.

A gap is forever

The first thing to notice is what "the counter on each side" means. It means the two places next to the one you clicked, not the nearest counters still standing — and turning over an empty place does nothing at all. So the hole you leave behind never closes, and it never conducts. Once a circle has holes in it, the counters that remain sit in separate arcs, and nothing you do in one arc can ever reach another. The puzzle quietly becomes several smaller, independent puzzles, each one a straight line of counters with a gap at both ends.

One arc, one parity test

Count the greens in a single arc. That count's parity — odd or even — decides everything:

An arc with a gap at each end can be cleared exactly when the number of greens in it is odd.

Both halves of that follow by induction on the length of the arc. If the count is odd, click the leftmost green: it has no greens to its left and therefore an even number on each side. Taking it away splits the arc in two and turns over the inside end of each half, which makes each half odd — so each half can be cleared the same way, all the way down.

If the count is even, every move fails, and it fails in the same way each time. Removing a green leaves two halves whose green counts add up to an odd number, and the two end-flips keep that sum odd, so one half is bound to come out even. "Some arc has an even number of greens" is therefore an invariant: once it is true it stays true forever. A cleared board has no arcs at all, so it can never be reached. An arc with no greens is the plainest case — zero is even, and there is nothing left to click.

Which circles can be cleared

A fresh circle has no gaps, so the arc test does not apply to it yet. But the first move makes it apply. On a full circle both neighbours of your counter are occupied, so that move changes the green count by −1 ± 1 ± 1, which is always odd: the parity of the green count always flips. One move in, you hold a single arc whose green count is odd exactly when the circle started out even. Add the obvious requirement that you need at least one green to move at all, and the whole puzzle collapses to a glance:

A circle can be cleared exactly when its number of greens is even and not zero.

That is the result Bogomolny's page proves, published in the American Mathematical Monthly in November 1997. Note how little it cares about the arrangement: it is only the number of greens that matters, not where they sit.

Try this: count the greens before your first click. If the number is odd, no sequence of moves can save the circle — take one counter anyway and watch the arcs get stuck one by one.
