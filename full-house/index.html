Full House

Puzzle by Erich Friedman, presented by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: draw one path that goes through every white square exactly once. Once the path is moving it keeps going until something stops it — the edge, a dark square, or the path itself.
How to play: click a square to start, then click a square to one side of the path's head to send it that way. It always slides as far as it can go.
Squares covered: 0 of 23
Look for the math

The rule that the path cannot stop where it likes is what turns a doodle into a puzzle. It also makes the puzzle small enough to think your way through, and it leaves two tests you can run on a board before you touch it.

You only ever choose a turn

You never choose how far to go, only which way to turn, and even that is a choice between two. At the end of a slide the path cannot carry straight on — that is why it stopped — and it cannot go back along itself, so only left and right are left. A route with t turns is one of at most 2t routes, and a 5×5 board needs around ten turns. That is a puzzle you can search by hand, which is exactly what the designer is counting on.

Colour the board like a chessboard

Every step of the path moves to a square of the other colour, so the path alternates all the way along: light, dark, light, dark. A path covering n squares therefore uses equal numbers of each colour when n is even, and one extra of its starting colour when n is odd. Count the white squares of each colour on your board:

If the two colours differ by more than one, no path can cover the board — whatever the sliding rule says.

And when they differ by exactly one, the path has to start and finish on the colour there is more of. Every board in this applet passes the test, of course; try it on a board of your own and you can throw out most blocked-square patterns without drawing anything. Covering every square of a grid once is a Hamiltonian path, and this colouring argument is the standard way to prove one cannot exist.

Every slide is a cut

You lose by stranding squares, not by running out of room. The path is one unbroken run, so the squares it has not reached yet must stay in one connected piece — and the piece has to be the one the head is standing next to. A long slide across the middle of the board cuts what is left into two islands, and that is the move that loses, several turns before you notice. This is why good play hugs the edges early: a slide along a wall takes squares away without splitting anything.

Try this: before each slide, ask what shape the uncovered squares will be left in. If they fall into two groups, step back and turn the other way.
