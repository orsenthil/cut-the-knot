Torque

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org) — the original "Achieve the Equilibrium" widget was Flash, courtesy Umapalata.com. Flash hasn't run in a browser since 2021, and what's left of its code is deliberately obfuscated, so this is a fresh build on the same balancing idea rather than a port of it.

Game: hang every weight and balloon on the beam so it balances perfectly level.
How to play: tap an item below, then tap an empty peg to hang it there (tap a hung item to take it back). Once every item is hung, press "Verify".
Solved: 0
Look for the math

A balanced beam is one of the oldest ideas in physics and one of the cleanest ways to feel arithmetic with signed numbers in your hands. Every item you hang on this beam contributes a torque (also called a moment) equal to its weight times its distance from the middle — and the beam is level exactly when all those torques, added up with sign, cancel to zero.

The lever principle

Archimedes is credited with the first clean statement of it: a weight w hung at distance d from the pivot turns the beam with "force" w × d. Put two items on opposite sides and the beam balances exactly when w1d1 = w2d2 — the classic lever law, and the reason a small child can lift an adult on a see-saw by sitting further from the middle.

Signed distance does the bookkeeping

This puzzle has more than two items, so it's easier to let distance carry a sign: pegs left of the pivot are negative, pegs right of it are positive, and a "balloon" is just a negative weight (it lifts instead of pulling down). Add up every item's value × position and equilibrium is simply:

value1·pos1 + value2·pos2 + … = 0

That single equation is doing the same job as separately balancing "pull left" against "pull right" — the signs sort it out automatically, the same trick that makes a number line useful in the first place.

Why the joker is always worth exactly 1

Every round includes one item worth exactly ±1 unit. With only big weights available, the sum of torques you can reach jumps in big, uneven steps, and zero might not be one of the stops. A ±1 piece is the finest adjustment there is: moved one peg over, it changes the total by exactly 1 — enough to patch up whatever parity the bigger items left behind, however far off they happen to leave you. It's the same role a 1-unit weight plays on a real balance scale, and the same idea behind why having a "1" lets a counting system reach every whole number instead of skipping some.

Many right answers

A round is only guaranteed to have a solution; it doesn't promise just one. If you find an arrangement that balances, it's correct even if it's not the one the generator originally had in mind — there's usually more than one way to zero out the sum.

Watch the beam tilt as you place items, before you press "Verify" — it leans toward whichever side is currently heavier, which is often enough to tell you where the next item should go.
