Hanoing

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: paint the whole row one colour. White always wins, and from the all-red start blue wins too.
How to play: click a square to repaint it. It may only change colour if it is the leftmost square of the colour it is losing, and ends up the leftmost square of the one it gains.
Moves: 0 Best possible from here: —
Look for the math

Nothing here looks like a tower. There are no discs, no pegs and no stacking — just a row of coloured squares and a rule about being leftmost. And yet this is the Tower of Hanoi, exactly, with the same best play and the same number of moves. Finding the disguise is the puzzle.

Reading the row as a tower

Here is the dictionary. Square i, counting from the left, is disc i — the leftmost square is the smallest disc. The square's colour is the peg that disc is sitting on. That is all; the position of the row is the position of the tower. Now read the two rules again:

leftmost of the colour it loses = smallest disc on its peg = on top, so you may lift it
leftmost of the colour it gains = smaller than everything there = you may drop it on that peg

The disguise works because a peg never needs remembering: discs on a peg are always stacked smallest first, so the single list "which peg is each disc on" is the whole state. Tick Show the towers to watch the two pictures move together.

Why it takes 2n − 1 moves

To get n discs onto a target peg, the biggest one has to move, and before it can, the other n−1 must be piled out of the way on the third peg. Then the big one moves once. Then that pile of n−1 has to be rebuilt on top of it. So if T(n) is the cost,

T(n) = 2·T(n−1) + 1,   T(0) = 0

and induction turns that recurrence into T(n) = 2n − 1: adding one square doubles the work and then some. Four squares take 15 moves, ten take 1023, and twenty take 1,048,575.

The best move from a muddle

Randomize drops you somewhere in the middle, and the recursion above no longer applies — but the same reasoning does, and it gives the exact answer rather than an estimate. Work down from the largest disc. If it is already where it needs to be, ignore it and ask again about the rest. If it is not, everything smaller has to clear onto the one remaining peg, which is the same question with a different goal, and then you pay 1 + (2i − 1) for the move itself and the rebuild. That loop is what fills in Best possible from here, and Suggest move plays its first step — which is why following the hints never wastes a move.

Try this: set 4 squares, solve it by hand, and watch Best possible from here. If it ever goes up, your last click was a detour. Getting from 15 to 0 without it ever rising is a perfect game.
