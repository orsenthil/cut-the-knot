Hex Can't End in a Draw

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: red wants a path from top to bottom, blue a path from left to right. Try to colour the board so that nobody wins.
How to play: click a cell to swap its colour. The dots show the winning path. Then try the square grids.
Look for the math

Hex has a remarkable property: however the board is filled, exactly one player has a winning path. It sounds obvious. It isn't, and the square grids show why.

Why there is always a winner

Suppose blue has no left-to-right path. Take every blue cell that is joined to the left edge. The cells that border that region on its right can't be blue (they would be in it), so they are red, and they link up into a red path from top to bottom. A board where nobody wins can't exist. David Gale showed that this fact is equivalent to the two-dimensional Brouwer fixed-point theorem. A check of every board up to 4 by 4, and of thousands of random boards up to 10 by 10, finds exactly one winner each time.

Gale's walk

Tick "Separation edges" to see Gale's proof run. The walk starts at the top-left corner, where the red edge meets the blue one, and always follows a side with red on one hand and blue on the other. At every corner of the honeycomb three cells meet, so there is exactly one way on. The walk can never come back to a corner it has used, so it must come out at another corner of the board, and the cells along it contain the winning path. The check confirms this on every board up to 4 by 4.

Squares need the right neighbours

With four neighbours per square, a draw is possible: a checkerboard pattern blocks both colours. With eight neighbours, a red and a blue path can cross at a diagonal and both win. These two failures are the same thing seen twice: red has no four-neighbour path exactly when blue has an eight-neighbour one. On a 4 by 4 board, 21,150 of the 65,536 colourings are draws on the 4-grid, and exactly those 21,150 are double wins on the 8-grid. Six neighbours (four sides and one diagonal) works like hexagons, and that is the grid John Nash used.

On the 4-grid, try to build a draw on a big board. Then switch to the 8-grid with the same idea and see both colours win.
