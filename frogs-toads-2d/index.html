Toads and Frogs in 2D

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: green frogs on one side, brown toads on the other, one empty square between them. Win by getting every frog onto a toad's square and every toad onto a frog's.
How to play: click an animal to move it. Frogs only go right or down, toads only go left or up, into the empty square or in one hop over a single animal of the other kind.
Look for the math

This is the old Toads and Frogs puzzle — also sold as Hares and Tortoises, or Traffic Jam — opened out into two dimensions. The flat version is a single row of squares, and it is worth meeting first, because the two-dimensional one is built out of copies of it.

The puzzle in a line, and its formula

Put N frogs on the left, M toads on the right, one gap between them. Every frog has to end up M+1 places to the right and every toad N+1 places to the left, which is N(M+1) + M(N+1) places of travel in all. A slide covers one place and a hop covers two, so the number of moves is that total minus the number of hops. Every frog must get past every toad exactly once, and the only way past is a hop, so there are exactly MN hops:

moves = N(M+1) + M(N+1) − MN = MN + M + N, of which MN are hops.

Set this board to one column (or one row) and you have exactly that puzzle. An exhaustive search over every position confirms the formula for all of them: 3 moves for one of each, then 8, 15 and 24.

Why the square board is so much cheaper

A 3 by 3 board holds four frogs and four toads — the same eight animals as a line of nine squares, which needs 24 moves. The square needs 12. In a line every frog has to hop over every single toad, because there is no other way past. On a grid a frog can go right or down, so animals can walk around one another and most of those 16 hops are never needed.

The shape of the board matters, not just its size

3 columns by 5 rows takes 22 moves; 5 columns by 3 rows takes 27, although both hold seven frogs and seven toads. The opening position is not symmetric: the middle column is frogs above the hole and toads below it, so turning the board on its side gives a genuinely different puzzle.

Solving it as a stack of line puzzles

Bogomolny's hint is to watch the middle column on its own. Every animal in it has to move, so the empty square must visit every square of that column — which is itself the one-dimensional puzzle. Each time the hole settles in a new row, that row is another one-dimensional puzzle waiting to be solved. Solving the column tells you which row puzzle to solve next.

Try one column with two frogs and two toads first (8 moves), and notice the rule that always works: when you can hop, hop; never leave two hops facing each other.
