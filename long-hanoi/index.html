Tower of Hanoi, the Hard Way

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: move the whole tower of disks from the left peg to the right peg, taking as many moves as you can.
How to play: click a peg to lift its top disk, then click another peg to drop it. Never put a disk on a smaller one, and never go back to a picture of the pegs you have already had.
Look for the math

The Tower of Hanoi is a race to use the fewest moves. Kai Birger Nielsen asked the opposite question: wasting moves is easy if you shuffle a disk back and forth, so ban that — never let the pegs look the way they have looked before — and then how long can you make the job take?

The longest route

In the short solution the biggest disk moves once, straight from the left peg to the right one. To stretch things out, send it the long way round, through the middle peg, and move the N−1 disks above it three times instead of two:

Solve(N, Src, Aux, Dst) = Solve(N−1, Src, Aux, Dst), Src→Aux, Solve(N−1, Dst, Aux, Src), Aux→Dst, Solve(N−1, Src, Aux, Dst)

The Slow and Fast buttons play exactly this. Notice that the middle peg is in every single move: no disk ever hops straight from the left peg to the right one.

Three to the N, minus one

Let TN be the length of that route. It moves N−1 disks three times and the big one twice, so

T0 = 0, TN = 3TN−1 + 2, which gives TN = 3N − 1.

That is the best anyone can do, and counting shows why. A picture of the pegs is just a list of which peg each disk is on, so with three pegs there are exactly 3N pictures. A route that never repeats one can visit at most all 3N of them, and 3N stops need only 3N−1 moves. The recursion hits that ceiling: played out to ten disks, it passes through every picture exactly once.

A triangle of positions

Draw a dot for every picture and join two dots when one move turns one into the other, and you get the Hanoi graph — the Sierpiński triangle drawn as a network, with the three all-on-one-peg pictures at its corners. The short solution runs down one side of the triangle. The long one is a Hamiltonian path: a walk that touches every dot once. That is also why you can get stranded. With two disks, four moves can park the tower on the middle peg with both ways out already used, and there is nothing to do but start again.

Knowing where you are

Because the long route passes through every picture, every picture has a number: how many moves along the route it is. You can read it off without replaying anything. Look at the biggest disk: on the left peg it is in the first third of the route, on the middle peg the second third, on the right peg the last third. Write down 0, 1 or 2 for that, then ask the same of the next disk down — swapping the left and right pegs if the digit was 1, because the middle third runs backwards. The digits spell the number in base 3, and "Suggest move" simply steps to the next number. The original applet's hint button and its closing message were both left over from the ordinary Tower of Hanoi it was built from: they pushed you towards the short solution, which is the wrong puzzle, so this page counts you against 3N−1 instead.

Try four disks by hand and see how close to 80 moves you get before you strand yourself. Then press "Suggest move" over and over and watch the counter stop at exactly 80.
