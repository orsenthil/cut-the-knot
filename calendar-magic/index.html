Calendar Magic

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: outline a square of dates on the calendar and circle one date in each row and each column of it. The computer tells you the total before you pick a single date, and it is never wrong.
How to play: click a date to put the square's top left corner there. Then click one date inside the square — never two in the same row or the same column — until the square is used up.

Look for the math

The prediction is not a guess and it is not a trick of arithmetic speed: the computer never looks at the dates you circle. It only looks at the square. Once you know why, you can perform this on paper with a real calendar, writing the total down in a sealed envelope before anyone picks anything.

A calendar is an arithmetic grid

Printed Sunday first, a month is one long run of numbers folded into rows of seven. So every line through it is an arithmetic progression: step right and the date goes up by 1, step down and it goes up by 7, step diagonally and it goes up by 8 or 6. Writing a for the date in the top left corner of your square, the date r rows down and c columns across is always

a + 7r + c

That one formula is the whole puzzle. Nothing about dates, months or leap years matters from here on — only the grid.

One from each row, one from each column

Now add up your picks. The rule says you use each row once and each column once, so your picks are the dates a + 7r + c where the row numbers r run through 0, 1, …, n−1 in some order, and so do the column numbers c — they are matched up by a permutation, and which permutation you chose is exactly what the sum forgets:

total = n·a + 7(0+1+…+(n−1)) + (0+1+…+(n−1)) = n·a + 4n(n−1)

For a 4×4 square that is 4a + 48, which is the number Bogomolny's page predicts. Every row contributes its 7r once and every column its c once, whatever order you pair them in, so there is nothing left for your choices to change. Picking one cell per row and per column is the same as placing non-attacking rooks, and the same as one symbol of a Latin square — which is how the trick generalises to sharing out presents fairly.

The same grid, a second trick

Three dates in a row, a column or a diagonal are a−d, a, a+d, so the middle one is the average of the outer two and the three of them add up to 3a. Write three such dates side by side as one long number and its digits add up to a multiple of 3, so by the usual divisibility rule the number itself is a multiple of 3. Multiply two of them and the product is a multiple of 9 — so its digits must add up to a multiple of 9 too.

Try this on paper: take 11, 12, 13 and 20, 21, 22, form 111213 and 202122, and multiply. Read the digits of the product out to a friend but skip one, and they can tell you the digit you skipped — whatever makes the digit sum reach the next multiple of 9.
