Changing Colors II

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: the board is all white except one black square. Try to make the whole board a single colour.
How to play: click an arrow to swap every colour along its row, column or slanting line.
Look for the math

However long you play, you never get one colour. Proving that something can never happen is the job of an invariant: a quantity that no move can change.

The hint cells

Tick "Show hint". The marked squares are the edge squares that are not corners. A middle row or column touches two of them, one at each end. The top row and bottom row, and the outside columns, touch N − 2 of them, an even number on these even boards. Every slanting line touches two of them at its ends, or none if it runs corner to corner or is a single corner square. So every move swaps an even number of hint squares, and the number of black ones among them stays odd or stays even, forever. It starts at one, so it is always odd, and one colour would need it to be 0 or all of them (also even).

The same idea with numbers

Switch to "numbers": black is −1, white is 1, and a move multiplies a line by −1. Each move changes an even number of hint squares, so their product doesn't change. It starts at −1, while a one-colour board would make it 1. This is parity in disguise.

Why only even boards?

On odd boards the top row touches an odd number of hint squares, so this argument breaks. Swaps are addition mod 2 and their order doesn't matter, so a computer can decide exactly which boards are reachable, by Gaussian elimination. It shows that 5 by 5 and 7 by 7 boards are impossible too, but they need a cleverer invariant. Only on a 3 by 3 board can one black edge square be cleared.

Watch the count of black hint squares as you play: it goes up and down, but it is always odd.
