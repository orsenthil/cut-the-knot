The Eye Opener: 99 = 100

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Size 10
Goal: fit the top piece into the square drawn above it.
How to play: drag the top piece one square up and one square right (or use the arrow keys). Then count the squares before and after.
Look for the math

You started with a rectangle 9 squares tall and 11 wide: 9 × 11 = 99 squares. You slid one piece and got a square 10 by 10: 100 squares. Sliding a shape never changes its area, so one square has to come from somewhere. This is a cousin of the famous missing square puzzle.

The steps are not whole squares

Look closely at the staircase. The rectangle is 9 squares tall, but the staircase climbs it in 10 steps, so each step is only 9/10 of a square high. That's why the step lines sit a little off the grid lines.

To make the stairs fit together, you move the top piece up by one step: 9/10 of a square. But to reach the top of the 10 × 10 square, it has to go up a full square. Honest pieces would leave a thin gap along the top, 1/10 of a square high.

Where the extra square comes from

The original applet quietly covers the gap. While you drag, it stretches the top of the piece by 1/10 of a square. The piece is 10 squares wide, so the stretch adds

10 × 1/10 = 1 whole square.

That's the 100th square. The part of the program that does it is even called CheatHere. Press Reveal the trick and lift the piece to see the sliver light up. For any size n, the steps are (n−1)/n high and the hidden strip is n squares long and 1/n tall: always exactly one square.

What the picture really proves

The picture still tells the truth about something. The rectangle has area (n−1)(n+1) and the square has area n². Split the rectangle into its first column and the rest, and split the square into its top row and the rest. Using the distributive law:

(n−1)(n+1) = (n−1) + (n−1)n
n × n = n + n(n−1)

The pieces (n−1)n and n(n−1) are the same. What's left over is a column of n−1 squares against a row of n squares, so the square is bigger by exactly one:

(n−1)(n+1) = n² − 1

That's the difference of two squares. It's why the sizes always come out as 24 = 25, 48 = 49, 99 = 100: one less than a perfect square against the square itself.

Pictures help, proofs decide

Bogomolny's point is that a drawing can suggest an idea, but only a proof settles it. A picture is safe to use only when you know the lengths exactly instead of judging them by eye. And once you accept 99 = 100, anything at all follows (logicians call this the principle of explosion). As a reader once joked, slide the piece down and left instead, and you get 98 = 99 = 100.

Try it: set the size to 5, so the claim is 4 × 6 = 5 × 5, that is 24 = 25. With fewer, bigger steps the trick is much easier to spot. Can you see the gap before you press Reveal?
