The Game of Jam

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: you and the computer take turns claiming roads. Win by owning every road that runs into one city.
How to play: click a road to claim it; yours turn blue and the computer's red. The game has a secret: can you find it?
Look for the math

John Michon's Jam looks like a game about a map, but it is a game everybody already knows. Two games that are the same game in disguise are called isomorphic.

The secret: tic-tac-toe

There are nine roads and eight cities, and every city has exactly three roads. Make each road a square of a 3 by 3 board and each city one of the eight lines: three rows, three columns, two diagonals. Owning all three roads into a city is owning a whole line. The middle road runs through four cities, like the centre square in four lines; four roads run through three, like the corners; four through two, like the edges. Tick "Show the secret" to watch both boards at once.

And a third disguise: adding to 15

Number the squares with the Lo Shu magic square:

4 9 2   3 5 7   8 1 6

The eight lines are exactly the eight ways to pick three different numbers from 1 to 9 that add up to 15. So "take turns picking numbers; first to hold three that add to 15 wins" is tic-tac-toe again. That game is called Fif. The applet carries this table inside it.

Can the computer be beaten?

With perfect play tic-tac-toe is a draw, so Jam is a stalemate when both sides know the secret. The page says the computer is one of those "in the know", making the odd "benevolent mistake". In fact it only looks one move ahead. It takes a win when it sees one and blocks yours, but it doesn't see forks, where you threaten two cities at once. Checking every game shows that, even with its mistakes switched off, you can always beat it when you move first, and also after two of its nine possible openings.

Moving first, take a corner road, then look for a move that threatens two cities at once.
