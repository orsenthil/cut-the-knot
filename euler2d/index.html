This page isn't a puzzle — it's a running simulation of a two dimensional incompressible, inviscid fluid, following the Euler equations. Stephen Montgomery-Smith wrote the original as the euler2d mode for the xlock/XScreenSaver screensaver collection; everything below follows the method in his euler2d.tex write-up.

Vorticity: the fluid's hidden scalar

Tracking the velocity of every point in a fluid directly is hard, because pressure couples every point to every other. It's easier to track the vorticity — a single number at each point measuring how much the fluid is locally spinning. For an inviscid 2D flow, vorticity has a beautifully simple life: it is just carried along by the flow, unchanged, forever. Once you know where the vorticity started and how the fluid moves, you know where the vorticity is at any later time.

The Biot–Savart kernel: velocity from vortices

This simulation starts with vorticity concentrated at a handful of points — think of them as the eyes of tiny hurricanes — each carrying a fixed strength wk. The Biot–Savart law turns that vorticity back into a velocity field that every point in the fluid — vortex or plain tracer — obeys:

u(x) = Σk wk (x − ak)⊥ / |x − ak|2

where ak is a vortex's position and v⊥ means v rotated a quarter turn. The "Power law" slider changes the exponent on the distance: the default, power 1, is the ordinary Biot–Savart law; power 2 gives the quasi-geostrophic equations used to model large-scale ocean and atmosphere currents, where the influence of a vortex fades faster with distance.

Keeping the flow inside the wall

A real fluid can't flow through its container's wall. On the unit disk, that boundary condition is enforced with a classic trick: for every vortex at a, add a mirror-image anti-vortex at its reflection a* = a / |a|2 just outside the disk. The two together cancel out exactly on the boundary circle, so the real fluid inside never crosses it. Watch a point drift close to the edge: it visibly curves away, repelled by its own mirror image.

A new boundary every run

A plain disk gets boring fast, so the "standard" power-law setting instead draws the domain as the image of the unit disk under a random conformal map p(z) = z + c2z2 + … + c6z6. Keeping Σ k|ck| = 1 guarantees p never folds the disk over itself, so the result is always a single simple blob — a different one each time you press "New flow". Points are scattered inside with density weighted by 1/|p′(z)|2 so that, once mapped through p, they look evenly spread over the oddly-shaped domain rather than bunched near its corners.

Marching forward in time

At every step the simulation adds up the pull of all the vortices on every point, then nudges each point along by a tiny amount. The very first step uses the midpoint method; every step after that switches to second-order Adams–Bashforth, which reuses the previous step's velocity to get a better estimate for less work — twice the speed of the fourth-order method the original program used before 2000. A point that gets flung too far in one step, or pushed outside the boundary by rounding error, is simply retired rather than left to blow up the whole simulation; a flow "ages out" and a fresh one begins once it has run for the chosen number of steps.

Try setting "Power law" to 2 and compare how tightly the vortices hold onto nearby points versus the default — that's the same mathematics behind why ocean eddies can persist for months.
