Pennies in Boxes

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: get every penny into a single box. Pick two boxes and the fuller one pays the other exactly as many pennies as that one is holding, so the smaller pile doubles.
How to play: click one box, then click another. Click the same box twice to change your mind.
Look for the math

Sixteen pennies always come together in the end, however they are dealt out. Twelve pennies almost never do. The difference is not luck, and you can see it before you make a move.

Every move doubles a box

Write the two chosen boxes as p and q with p ≥ q. After the move they hold

(p, q) → (p − q, 2q)

The total never changes, and the receiving box always doubles. That already rules out half of all deals: the very last move leaves one box holding everything, and it got there by doubling, so the total must be even. An odd number of pennies can never be gathered up.

Powers of two always work

Bogomolny's page proves the opposite extreme. If two boxes hold odd numbers, one move leaves both of them even — p − q and 2q are both even when p and q are odd. Pair the odd boxes off like that and every box holds an even number, so you can glue the pennies together in twos and play the same puzzle with 2n−1 double pennies instead. Repeat, and the deal shrinks to a single penny: that is induction on n. To empty one of three boxes x < y < z the page writes y = qx + r and reads the binary digits of q: each move doubles the small box to x, 2x, 4x, …, and taking each power out of y or z as the digits dictate leaves the remainder r < x. The remainders keep shrinking, which is infinite descent: it has to stop.

The test: divide by the common factor

Between "never" and "always" there is one clean rule. Let g be the greatest common divisor of the piles. Then:

the pennies can be gathered into one box exactly when total ÷ g is a power of two.

One way round is easy: divide every pile by g and the rule for a move is untouched, so a scaled-down deal with a power-of-two total can be gathered by the argument above, and scaling back up gathers the real one. The other way round is the gcd itself. If g' is the new gcd after a move then g' divides p − q and 2q, so it divides 2p too, and it divides twice every other pile for free — meaning g' divides 2g. The common factor can only stay put or double. One box holding everything has gcd N, so N must be g times a string of doublings. That is the test, and the Hint button uses it: it will tell you plainly when a deal is hopeless.

Try it: deal 12 pennies into three boxes. Only 3 + 3 + 6 can be gathered up — divide by 3 and it is 1 + 1 + 2, total 4. Every other split of 12 is a dead loss, and now you can say why without touching a penny.
