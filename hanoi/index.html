Tower of Hanoi

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: move the whole tower of disks from the left peg to the right peg.
How to play: click a peg to lift its top disk, then click another peg to drop it there. Move one disk at a time, and never put a disk on a smaller one.
Look for the math

Édouard Lucas invented this puzzle in 1883. It is the classic first example of recursion: a big problem solved by solving a smaller copy of itself.

The recursive solution

Sooner or later the biggest disk has to move from the left peg to the right one. At that moment every other disk must be stacked on the middle peg. So: move the top N−1 disks to the middle, move the big disk, then move the N−1 disks on top of it.

Solve(N, Src, Aux, Dst) = Solve(N−1, Src, Dst, Aux), Src→Dst, Solve(N−1, Aux, Src, Dst)

The Slow and Fast buttons play exactly this.

How many moves

Let TN be the fewest moves for N disks. The argument above says you must move N−1 disks twice and the big one once, so

T0 = 0, TN = 2TN−1 + 1, which gives TN = 2N − 1.

This is a recurrence relation. Add 1 to both sides and it doubles each time: TN+1 = 2(TN−1+1). Four disks take 15 moves and ten take 1,023. The legend's 64 disks, at one move a second, would take about 585 billion years.

A hint from anywhere

"Suggest move" doesn't replay the recursion. It works from any position, even a messy one, using an idea of Romek Zylla. Start with the biggest disk and the right peg as its target. If the disk is already there, look at the next disk down with the same target. If the disk is free to move onto its target, that's the move. Otherwise the disks above it have to get out of the way first, onto the third peg, so that peg becomes the next disk's target. Checking every one of the 3N positions for up to 8 disks, following the hints always finishes in the fewest possible moves.

Watch the smallest disk in a perfect solution: it moves every other turn, always around the pegs in the same direction.
