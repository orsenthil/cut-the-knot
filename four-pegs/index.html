Four Pegs

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: four pegs sit at the corners of a square — try to move them into a bigger square.
How to play: click a peg and it shows you where it can go. A peg may hop over another peg and land the same distance past it; the peg it hopped over stays put.
Look for the math

You can hop the pegs about for a long time without getting anywhere, and there is a reason for that: a bigger square is impossible. So is a smaller one, and so is a tilted one. The proof is two short steps, and the second is the kind of trick worth stealing.

A hop cannot leave the grid

Call a set of points a lattice if, whenever three corners of a parallelogram are in it, the fourth is too — in vectors, if a, b and c are in it then so is a + b − c. Every set of points sits inside a smallest lattice containing it. Now look at a hop: peg p jumps over peg q and lands at

p′ = 2q − p = q + q − p

which is exactly the parallelogram rule applied to q, q and p. So the new point was already in the smallest lattice containing the old ones — the lattice does not grow. And a hop undoes itself (jump straight back over the same peg), so it cannot shrink either. The smallest lattice is an invariant: it is the same after every move you will ever make. The status line prints its step, and you can watch it refuse to budge.

So the square can never change size

Start from a square of side 1. Its smallest lattice is the ordinary grid of whole-number points, and the closest two points of that grid are 1 apart — so no square smaller than the original is ever reachable. That is the easy half. The other half comes free:

Suppose some sequence of hops turned the side-1 square into a square of side s > 1. Run it backwards — every hop reverses — and it turns a side-s square into a side-1 one. Shrink that whole picture by a factor of s: hops are defined by midpoints, so a shrunk game is still a legal game. It takes a side-1 square to a square of side 1/s < 1, which the first half just ruled out.

So no bigger square, no smaller one, and the same argument in any direction kills the tilted ones: a square of side √2 would shrink to one of side 1/√2.

The fifth peg changes the game

Turn the fifth peg on with the side set to 2 and you may drop it on a point the other four could never reach. Their lattice gets finer — the status line will read step 1 by 2 — and the argument above loses its grip, because the pegs' grid is now finer than their square. That is not a technicality. Three hops are then enough to put the four original pegs on a square of side √8, twice the area of the one they started in:

The square sits at (5,5) (7,5) (7,7) (5,7); drop the helper at (6,5). Hop the (5,5) peg over (7,5) to (9,5), then back over the helper to (3,5), then hop (7,7) over the helper to (5,3). The four now sit on (3,5) (7,5) (5,3) (5,7) — a square of side √8.

One peg of help, and the impossible becomes a three-move exercise. What the search never turned up was a bigger square sitting square to the grid: every bigger one it found was tilted. The five-peg case has its own argument on Bogomolny's page, with a greatest common divisor and a count of grid points inside the square; follow the link under the title. With the side left at 1 the fifth peg does nothing at all, since every grid point is already in the pegs' own lattice.

Try this: set the side to 2, turn on the fifth peg and drop it one step to the right of a corner. The status line should say the pegs reach a grid of step 1 by 2 — then see how few hops you need to beat the square. Three is enough.
