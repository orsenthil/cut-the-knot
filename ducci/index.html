Integer Iterations on a Circle

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: numbers sit in a circle. Each step, every pair of neighbours is replaced by the gap between them. Will the circle end up all zeros?
How to play: press "One step" and watch. Click the right side of a number to add 1, the left side to take 1 away, and try other sizes.
Look for the math

This is E. Ducci's problem, and the sequences it makes are called Ducci sequences. Start with four numbers and you always end at four zeros. Change the number of numbers and something different happens.

Why four always dies out

The numbers never get bigger: a gap between two numbers is at most the larger of them. Now look at them only as even or odd. Whatever four numbers you start with, within four steps they are all even. Halve them all and the same argument applies again, so they become divisible by 4, then by 8, and so on. Numbers that never grow but are divisible by every power of 2 must be 0. Checking every start with numbers from 0 to 15 agrees, and also confirms Honsberger's observation that the largest number drops within four steps.

Powers of two, and everything else

The same parity argument works for 8, 16, 32 numbers: any power of 2 dies out. For other sizes it usually fails. Start with a single 1 among zeros: it reaches all zeros exactly when the number of numbers is a power of 2, which links to the even and odd entries of Pascal's triangle through Lucas' theorem. Some other starts still die out, like 1 0 1 0 1 0, 1 2 1 2 1 0 and 1 2 3 2 1 0 with six numbers.

Loops

When a circle doesn't die out, it falls into a loop, and how long the loop is depends on what you count as "the same". With five numbers the circle comes back turned round after 3 steps, on the same ring of positions after 6, and exactly as it was after 15. Bogomolny's page says six numbers give loops of length 2 and 3. Checking every start with numbers up to 3 gives a slightly different picture: there are two kinds of loop, one that repeats exactly after 6 steps and one after 3, or after 2 and 1 steps if you allow the circle to turn.

The absolute value matters. Without it (just a − b), four numbers that are not all equal grow without limit. Try three numbers too: 0 0 1 never dies out.
