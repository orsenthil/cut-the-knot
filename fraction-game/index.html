This is a browser version of Alexander Bogomolny's applet "Fraction Game", which is Conway's Contorted Fractions from Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays. Left may only make a number smaller, Right may only make it larger, and either way the denominator has to shrink. No move touches two fractions at once, so the table is several separate games played side by side — the setting of combinatorial game theory.

Your best move is always a parent

Left wants the number to drop as little as possible, so Left wants the largest number below x with a smaller denominator. That number has a name: it is x's left parent on the Stern–Brocot tree, and Right's best is the right parent. From 2/5:

Left should play 1/3, Right should play 1/2.

The two parents always add up to the fraction itself, numerator with numerator and denominator with denominator — (1+1)/(3+2) = 2/5. That sum is called the mediant, and it is how the whole tree is built. Every other move you could make is worse than a parent, so each fraction really offers just two moves: x = { left parent | right parent }.

What a fraction is worth

Two options, one for each player, is exactly the shape of a number in game theory. Its worth is the simplest number lying strictly between what the two parents are worth, and an integer is simply worth itself. Working upward:

1/2 = { 0 | 1 }, worth 1/2.
1/3 = { 0 | 1/2 }, worth 1/4.
2/5 = { 1/3 | 1/2 }, worth 1/4 to 1/2, so 3/8.

Those worths are always dyadic — halves, quarters, eighths — even though the fractions on the table are not. Add them up over the whole table and you know everything: a positive total means Left wins no matter who starts, a negative total means Right does, and a total of exactly 0 means whoever moves second wins. Tick "Show values" to watch the sum.

Where the computer goes wrong

The applet's opponent is greedy in the obvious way: of all the parents available, it plays the one that shifts its fraction the least. That is nearly always right, and it is wrong for a reason worth seeing — what matters is not how far the number moves but how far its worth moves. Take the table −3/5, 1/5, 3/5, worth 1/16 in total, so Left to play wins:

−3/5 → −2/3 moves the number by only 0.067, but its worth by 1/8.
1/5 → 0 moves the number by 0.2, three times as far, but its worth by just 1/16.

Greedy takes the first and hands the total to −1/16, a loss. The second leaves 0 with Right to move, which Left wins. Sweeping every two- and three-fraction table with numbers up to 6, the port's test finds the opponent keeps 1,853 of the 1,855 wins it is handed — and this is one of the two it drops.

Tick "Show values", take Left, and press "Computer Moves" whenever the total is negative: you are already lost, so make it work for the win.
