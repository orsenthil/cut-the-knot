Flip Them

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Goal: flip every triangle so it points down.
How to play: click any M triangles — they flip together.
Look for the math

This is a browser version of Alexander Bogomolny's "Flipping Items Simultaneously", which starts from a specific case: 7 triangles, flipping 4 at a time. However you choose your moves, you can never turn every triangle downward. Try it above with M = 4, N = 7 — then read on for why it's hopeless.

An invariant: the sign of a product

Give every triangle the value +1 when it points up and −1 when it points down, and look at the product Π of all N values. All-up starts at Π = 1; the goal, all-down, is Π = (−1)N. Flipping a triangle just multiplies Π by −1, so flipping M of them at once multiplies Π by (−1)M:

"If M is even then flipping M triangles does not affect Π."

Π staying fixed forever is exactly the kind of invariant that decides a puzzle before you've made a single move: with N = 7 (odd) the target needs Π to flip sign, but an even M can never change it. That's the whole proof that M = 4, N = 7 is impossible — and more generally, "when N is odd and M is even, the puzzle is unsolvable."

When M and N share no factors

Bogomolny credits the general result to a "well known" fact about combining moves of size M until you effectively can flip any single triangle. When M and N are coprime (share no common factor), the picture is completely settled:

"When M and N are mutually prime, the puzzle is solvable wherever M is odd, and unsolvable otherwise."

Try M = 3, N = 7 (coprime, M odd) and it falls quickly; M = 2, N = 7 (coprime, M even) never does, for the same parity reason as above.

The near-total flip: M = N − 1

A cute special case uses counting combinations directly instead of the parity trick:

"If M = N − 1, there are C(N, N−1) = N combinations of N−1 elements out of N. Each of N elements enters N−1 of the combinations. Carrying out the flips corresponding to all N combinations will turn each of the N triangles N−1 times, which is an odd number [when N is even]."

So for M = N−1, the puzzle is solvable when N is even and unsolvable when N is odd — the mirror image of the mutual-primality case above, because now gcd(M, N) = gcd(N−1, N) = 1 always, but M's parity runs opposite to N's.

How many moves does it take?

When a configuration is solvable at all, Bogomolny notes it doesn't take long:

"For odd N, the problem can always be solved in 3 steps. When N is even, one needs at least 4 moves."

Try it yourself: N = 7, M = 4 never finishes no matter how you play; switch to N = 7, M = 3 and it's over in three clicks of three triangles each.
