Rooster, Hen, Farmer and Wife

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Who's playing
Board size
How to play: move the farmer and wife one square at a time (drag them, or click one and press the arrow keys). Then the rooster and hen each hop one square to try to escape. Trap them so they have nowhere left to hop!
Look for the math

This is a browser version of one of Sam Loyd's puzzles, as retold by Alexander Bogomolny on cut-the-knot.org. The surprising part isn't the puzzle's rules — it's that on an 8×8 board, no amount of skill lets the farmer catch the rooster, or the wife catch the hen. The only winning pairings are the crossed ones: farmer vs. hen, and wife vs. rooster. That's exactly why this page's "Farmer & Rooster" and "Wife & Hen" modes are the two you can never finish, while "Rooster & Wife" and "Farmer & Hen" always can.

Measuring distance on a grid

Everyone here moves one step at a time, orthogonally — never diagonally. The natural way to measure "how far apart" two squares are under that rule is taxicab (city-block) distance: the number of grid steps between them, not a straight line through the middle. For squares (r1, c1) and (r2, c2):

dist(P, Q) = |r1 − r2| + |c1 − c2|

On the original 8×8 board, the farmer starts 2 steps from the rooster and 5 steps from the hen (the wife's numbers mirror this the other way around):

"At the outset, dist(rooster, farmer) = 2, dist(farmer, hen) = 5."

The parity argument

A capture can only happen at the instant a human steps onto a bird's square — which means the distance between them was exactly 1 right before that move. Every single step, by anyone, changes the taxicab distance between two pieces by exactly 1 (never by 0 or 2), so it flips the parity of that distance — odd becomes even, even becomes odd. In one full round the human moves once and, if it wasn't caught, the bird moves once too, so the distance gets flipped twice and lands back on the parity it started with:

"Unless it's captured, a move by a chicken restores the parity of the distance between it and the pursuing human."

That makes the starting parity an invariant of the chase: it can never change, round after round. Since capture requires the distance to be the odd number 1, a pairing that starts at an even distance (farmer–rooster: 2, wife–hen: 2) can never be captured — the parity simply won't allow it, no matter how many moves are played. A pairing that starts at an odd distance (farmer–hen: 5, wife–rooster: 5) is exactly the reverse: with the right play it's only a matter of time before the bird runs out of safe squares and is forced onto the human's square. On the classic board, Loyd's solution catches the first bird on move 8 and the second on move 9.

This whole setup — two sides taking turns, one chasing, one fleeing, on a board — is a small, fully-solved example of a pursuit–evasion game. What makes this one worth remembering isn't a clever chase pattern; it's that the outcome is decided before the first move is even made, purely by the parity of the starting distance.

Try it yourself: switch to "Farmer & Rooster" or "Wife & Hen" above and play for a while — then switch to "Farmer & Hen" or "Rooster & Wife" and watch how quickly it ends instead.
