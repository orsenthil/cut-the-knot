Looping Chips

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: four coloured chips sit on a loop of twelve squares. Put them back on those first four squares, but in reverse order — or decide that it cannot be done.
How to play: tap a chip, then tap an empty square five places away along the loop. The ring on the right is the same board, drawn so each move is just a step to a neighbour.

Look for the math

The same idea that unlocks Four Knights works here. From every square there are only two legal jumps, and chaining those jumps visits every square exactly once before returning — one big cycle.

A jump of five is a step of one

Number the squares 0 through 11 around the left-hand loop. A legal move adds or subtracts 5 (mod 12). Now list the squares in the order 0, 5, 10, 3, 8, 1, 6, 11, 4, 9, 2, 7 — that is (i × 5) mod 12, which is exactly how the applet builds its right-hand board. On that list, each jump of five becomes a step to the next or previous entry. The ring you see on the right is that list drawn in a circle.

Chips that cannot pass

On the right-hand ring a move is just sliding a chip into an empty neighbouring slot. Chips never jump over each other, so the order of the four colours around the ring never changes — only which gaps sit between them. That cyclic order is an invariant of the puzzle.

Why reverse is impossible

At the start the colours read, walking the right-hand ring from square 1, red, gray, yellow, blue. The reverse-order goal on the home squares would read gray, red, blue, yellow — the same four chips walking the other way. No sequence of neighbour-steps can flip the orientation of a cycle, so the goal is unreachable. Checking every reachable position (there are 4 × C(12,4) = 1980 of them) confirms none reverse the home squares.

The page also notes that when gcd(N, L) > 1 the right-hand board splits into several separate loops (for L = 4 there would be four loops of three). Here gcd(12, 5) = 1, so there is just one loop — and that is already enough to prove the puzzle cannot be solved.

Turn on Hint and watch a chip's number on the left match its place on the right. Once you trust the two boards are the same graph, the invariant is hard to unsee.
