3-Colour Tower of Hanoi

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: three towers, red, gold and blue. Win by rebuilding all three, each on a different peg from the one it started on.
How to play: click a peg to lift its top disk, then click another peg to drop it there. A disk can never go on a smaller one, but it may go on one of the same size.
Look for the math

John Mason pointed out that this puzzle can be solved if the goal is a derangement: every tower ends somewhere it didn't start. With three towers there are only two derangements, the two ways of turning the colours one peg round. The Slow, Fast and One step buttons play an algorithm by Stuart Anderson.

Gather, shift, spread

To swap the biggest disks, every smaller disk must first be out of the way in one pile. So Anderson works with a few building blocks. SHIFT moves a pile of size-matched triples from one peg to another, just like the ordinary Tower of Hanoi but three disks at a time. GATHER collects all the disks into one pile, and SPREAD undoes it. The whole solution is

GATHER, SHIFT, SHIFT, SPREAD

The two SHIFTs carry the gathered pile one peg round. SPREAD then deals the disks back out exactly as GATHER collected them, but from a rotated starting point, so each colour lands one peg along.

Is it the shortest way?

Anderson wrote that his method "may not be optimal". A computer search of every position settles it for small towers: it isn't.

He also guessed that his building blocks only work for some orders of the pegs. In fact all six orders work: three of them give one derangement and three give the other.

The applet's finish line

Bogomolny's applet only checked that no peg held a disk of its own starting colour, so mixed-up piles counted as a win (one disk of each colour "solves" in 4 moves). This version asks for what the puzzle states: three whole single-colour towers.

Try one disk of each colour first. Five moves is the best possible. Can you find them?
