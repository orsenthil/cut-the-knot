Gergonne's Magic Trick

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: think of one of the 27 numbers and keep it to yourself. After three questions the computer will tell you exactly which one it is.
How to play: click the button beside the row your number is in. The numbers get gathered up and dealt again; do it three times and watch.
Look for the math

There is no guessing here and nothing is hidden from you. The three answers you give are enough, all by themselves, to pin down one number out of twenty-seven — and the trick is arranged so that the answer always finishes in the same seat. It is named after Joseph Diez Gergonne, who analysed it in 1813.

Each answer divides the field by three

Before you say anything, your number could be any of the 27. Your first answer rules out two rows and leaves 9. The numbers are gathered and dealt out again, which spreads those 9 across all three rows — three in each — so your second answer leaves 3. Deal again and those 3 are spread one per row, so your third answer leaves exactly 1:

27 → 9 → 3 → 1

That is the real content of the trick. Three answers, each with three possible values, carry 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 different messages, which is precisely enough to name one number out of 27. Nothing is left over, and nothing is missing.

Why it is always the middle one

Knowing which number is left is not the same as knowing where it is, and that is the sleight of hand. When the rows are gathered up, the row you pointed at is always picked up second — sandwiched in the middle — so after the gather your number sits somewhere in the middle third of the stack, whatever you answered. Deal that stack out again and watch where it can be. In the real order of the rows, which the shuffling hides from you until the end:

after one answer: one of the middle three places of its row
after two: the middle place of its row, exactly
after three: the middle place of the middle row

Each round squeezes it one step further in, and the third leaves nowhere else to be: counting across the rows, the 14th of the 27. The computer does not so much deduce your number as walk it into a chair.

Three rows is not an accident

The applet deals N = 3 rows of NN−1 = 9, which is NN = 27 numbers in all, and asks N questions. Those sizes have to match: N answers of N kinds each can distinguish NN numbers and no more. But the counting is not the whole story — the gather has to put the named row in a true middle, so it also wants an odd number of rows. Simulating the same procedure shows it corners the number for 3 rows and for 5, and fails outright for 4.

Try answering the same row three times on purpose, if your number lets you. The gather barely moves anything — and the trick still works, because it never needed your answers to be interesting, only to be true.
