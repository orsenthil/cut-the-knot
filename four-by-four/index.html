Four By Four

A Java3D demo applet by Sun Microsystems, hosted (not authored) by Alexander Bogomolny at cut-the-knot.org.

Game: three-dimensional tic-tac-toe on a 4×4×4 cube. Be first to get four marks in a straight line — along any row, any column, any diagonal, through any layer.
How to play: the cube is shown as its four horizontal layers, bottom to top. Tap any empty circle to play there; the computer replies right away.

Look for the math

This one isn't a cut-the-knot original: it's a Java3D sample program Sun Microsystems shipped with its own developer kit, which Bogomolny simply hosts. It's ordinary tic-tac-toe stretched into a third dimension — a 4×4×4 cube instead of a 3×3 square — and that extra dimension changes the counting in interesting ways.

76 ways to win

In flat 3×3 tic-tac-toe there are 8 winning lines. Stretch the board into a cube and let lines run in any of the 13 directions a straight line can take through a 3D grid (axis-aligned, face-diagonal, or the four long diagonals through the whole cube) and the count jumps to 76: 48 straight rows (3 directions × 16 each), 24 face diagonals (3 axis-pairs × 4 layers × 2 diagonals), and 4 space diagonals corner-to-corner through the middle. The game's own instructions point out that the 8 outer corners and the 8 cells of the very centre each sit on exactly 7 of those 76 lines — more than anywhere else on the board — which is why both are worth grabbing early.

An AI built from carpentry, not lookahead

This computer opponent never searches ahead the way a minimax chess engine does. It's a stack of named, hand-built pattern checks — "is there an open three anywhere?", "do two of my opponent's pairs cross at an empty cell?", "do three corners of a face plus one more piece make the exact shape I call a chair?" — tried in order, first match wins. Each skill level simply uses a shorter or longer list of checks, from a handful at the easiest setting up to the full stack at the hardest.

The game's own instructions are refreshingly honest about what that approach costs: "there are... faults in its logic that can be exploited. Thus the human player can win even at the highest skill level." A fixed list of patterns can only ever catch the shapes someone thought to name.

Scoring a win

Beating the computer scores skill × 1311 + (66 − moves) × 111 − min(seconds × 1000, 5000) — the original's own formula. Harder opponents, fewer total moves, and a faster finish are each worth more.

Try the hardest setting and aim for one of the 8 corner cells or the 8 centre cells on your first move — the instructions call them "the most winning combinations" for a reason.
