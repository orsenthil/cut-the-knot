Hex 7

Puzzle by Alexander Bogomolny (cut-the-knot.org)

Game: you and the computer take turns colouring hexagons. White wins by joining the top and bottom edges with a chain of white, Black by joining the left and right edges with black.
How to play: click an empty hexagon to take it. White goes first, and the very first move can't be the middle hexagon.
Look for the math

Hex was invented by Piet Hein in 1942 and again by John Nash in 1948. The rules are tiny, but two famous facts sit behind them.

Nobody can draw

Fill the whole board and exactly one player has a winning chain. If White has no top-to-bottom chain, the black cells along the edge of White's blocked region form a left-to-right chain, and the other way round. David Gale showed this fact is equivalent to the two-dimensional Brouwer fixed-point theorem. A check of every full board up to 4 by 4, and of 200,000 random full 7 by 7 boards, finds exactly one winner every time.

Going first is an advantage

Nash's strategy-stealing argument shows the first player can always win. Suppose the second player had a winning strategy. The first player makes any move, then pretends to be second and follows that strategy. An extra stone of your own never hurts in Hex, so the first player would win too, which is impossible. The argument says a winning strategy exists without saying what it is. Searching the whole game tree confirms it for every board from 1 by 1 up to 4 by 4.

That is why the middle hexagon is blocked for the first move here: it is the strongest opening. Even so, the first player still wins on a 3 by 3 board with the middle blocked.

How the computer plays

Because a full board always has a winner, a move can be judged by random play-outs: try the move, fill the rest of the board at random many times, and count how often you win. The computer does this for every empty hexagon and takes the best one, after first taking a winning hexagon or blocking yours. "Basic" plays a dozen random games per hexagon, "Advanced" 1,500.

Look for the "bridge": two of your stones with two empty hexagons between them. If your opponent takes one, you take the other, and the two stones stay connected.
