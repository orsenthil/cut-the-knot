Look for the math

<<<<<<< HEAD This is the Tower of Hanoi with every disk twinned: two of each size, one gold and one red, dealt out as two striped towers. It was set for 8-to-12-year-olds at the 1988 French championship of mathematical games, and the one extra rule is that a disk may rest on another of exactly its own size. That sounds like a kindness. It is really what the puzzle is built on.

Twinning the disks only doubles the work

Shifting an ordinary tower of k disks from one peg to another takes 2k − 1 moves. Shift a doubled pile — two disks of each of k sizes — and each pair travels as a unit: move the 2(k−1) smaller disks aside, move the two big twins one after the other onto the same peg, bring the small ones back. So T(k) = 2·T(k−1) + 2, which gives

T(k) = 2k+1 − 2 — exactly twice the ordinary tower.

That is why the bottom disk is the expensive one. To move it at all, every smaller disk has to be piled up out of the way on a single other peg.

Separating the colours is the easy half

If all you had to do was sort the colours, you could leave the two biggest disks exactly where they are: the gold giant already sits at the bottom of the left peg, so bring the golds to it and the reds to the other peg. With three sizes that takes only 10 moves. The puzzle asks for more — the towers must trade pegs — so both giants must move, each one paying the full T price above. Three sizes now costs 29 moves. Almost all of the difficulty is in that swap, not in the sorting.

The shortest solution, and a published one that is a move too long

A breadth-first search over every position this game can reach settles the question for small boards. The fewest possible moves are:

1 size: 3   2: 10   3: 29   4: 67   5: 147   6: 310

No tidy formula is known for them. The solution page cut-the-knot links to, contributed by a reader, derives a general formula and gives a 30-move answer for three sizes, ending with the honest question "Is that right?". This port's own test replays that sequence and confirms it: every move is legal and it does finish the puzzle. It is simply one move longer than it needs to be.

Start with one size. Three moves, and you can feel the whole puzzle in them: the only way to swap two disks is to park one on the spare peg. Then try two sizes, and watch where the spare peg goes. ======= The classic Tower of Hanoi is the textbook example of a recurrence relation: a problem that solves itself by calling on a smaller copy of itself. Doubling the rings and giving them colours breaks that tidy answer in an interesting way.

The classic count

To shift a tower of n rings to another peg, move the top n−1 out of the way, move the big one across, then bring the n−1 back on top. So

T(n) = 2·T(n−1) + 1, and with T(0) = 0 that gives T(n) = 2n − 1.

Every extra ring doubles the work and adds one. The famous 64-ring legend works out at 264 − 1 moves, which is why the monks are taking their time.

Why two towers are not just twice the work

Here there are two rings of every size, starting on different pegs, and the rule that a ring may rest on another of its own size. Two separate Hanois would be 2(2n − 1) = 2n+1 − 2 moves — but you cannot run them separately, because each tower is in the other's way, and every size has to be reunited somewhere. Searching every position of the puzzle gives the real minimums:

n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8
least moves = 2, 7, 19, 44, 96, 201, 413, 838

Three rings a side takes 19 moves, not the 14 that two separate towers would suggest. The numbers fit their own recurrence, still doubling each time but with a bigger correction term:

a(n) = 2·a(n−1) + ⌈3n/2⌉, with a(1) = 2.

That is a pattern read off the search rather than something proved here — it holds for every n up to 9, which is one past the largest tower this page offers.

A harder question

Bogomolny's page actually asks something else, and something harder: leave the rings in two towers but make each tower a single colour, the big rings swapping pegs. That version was set at the 1988 French mathematical games championship and needs far more moves — 30 for three rings a side rather than 19. The applet this page is built from asks for the pile-them-all-up version, so that is the one you are playing.
